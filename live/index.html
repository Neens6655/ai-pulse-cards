Anthropic is selling safety to Wall Street — and the math actually works

Anthropic has confidentially filed for a Nasdaq IPO at a rumoured $2 trillion valuation while simultaneously lobbying for an industry slowdown — a contradiction that is, on closer inspection, a strategy. Safety is the product differentiation, not the brake. If regulators bite and rivals are forced to slow down, Anthropic's cautious positioning becomes a moat. The risk is that Wall Street, and a deregulation-minded White House, won't play along.

The Desk
Why it mattersAbu Dhabi is a significant Anthropic backer; a $2 trillion IPO forces a decision on whether to hold, double down, or diversify into the next frontier lab.
The big pictureIf Washington pushes back on any slowdown, the labs that moved fastest — and their backers — absorb the upside; safety-first positioning becomes a liability, not a moat.

The full story

Anthropic is five years old, has never turned a profit, and is preparing to list on Nasdaq at a valuation that would make it one of the largest technology companies on earth. At the same time, its CEO Dario Amodei is publicly calling for the industry to pump the brakes on capability development. Both things are happening simultaneously, and the instinct is to call this a contradiction. It is not.

The actual play is simpler: Anthropic is attempting to write the rules of the game it intends to win. If regulators in Washington, Brussels, or London adopt meaningful safety thresholds — mandatory third-party audits, compute caps, incident reporting requirements — every lab that pushed capabilities fastest faces retroactive liability or forced redesign. Anthropic, which has built its entire brand around Constitutional AI and responsible scaling policies, would face the lightest burden and carry the most credible enterprise sales pitch. Safety, in this reading, is not the brake. It is the barrier to entry.

What most observers are getting wrong is treating the IPO and the slowdown call as separate stories. They are one move. The IPO prospectus needs a differentiation story that justifies a valuation multiple no pure-revenue model can support. "We are the safe one" is that story — for institutional investors nervous about AI liability, for government procurement officers, and for large enterprises that cannot afford a reputational incident. OpenAI is selling speed; Anthropic is selling insurance. Both are enormous markets.

The risk is the White House. The Bloomberg reporting makes clear that Trump administration officials are openly hostile to any industry slowdown, viewing AI acceleration as a geopolitical imperative against China. If Washington actively blocks safety-based regulation, Anthropic's moat narrows sharply. A deregulated race benefits whoever has the most compute and the most aggressive release cadence — neither of which is Anthropic's comparative advantage.

For Abu Dhabi, the stakes are direct. Gulf sovereign capital is already inside Anthropic's cap table, and a $2 trillion public float would be one of the defining liquidity events in technology investment history. The question is not whether to celebrate — it is whether to use the IPO window to rebalance. A $2 trillion valuation prices in regulatory outcomes that are, at this moment, genuinely uncertain. If the Trump administration succeeds in neutering safety regulation, the valuation story weakens and the labs with more raw compute and faster release cycles — many of them backed by or supplying Gulf infrastructure — capture the upside instead.

The specific watch item: how Anthropic prices its IPO relative to its most recent private round, and whether the government-sales momentum holds. OpenAI just ended its $1-per-year federal deal and moved agencies to a 50% discount — a signal that the U.S. government AI budget is real and growing. Whoever locks in the largest federal contract base before the IPO window closes sets the revenue floor that justifies the multiple. That race is happening now.

U.S. workers' pay is being pressured. AI is drawing more attention

New data from Apollo Global Management show that workers in roles highly exposed to AI saw real-wage growth roughly **6.7 percentage points** slower than their peers after 2023 — yet employment levels barely budged. Inflation-adjusted wages are already down 0.4% year-on-year, and labor's share of economic output sits at a multi-decade low. AI's first measurable economic toll may be landing on paychecks, not headcounts. That distinction matters enormously for how governments and employers respond.

CNBC
Why it mattersIf AI compresses wages before it eliminates jobs, the policy playbook — retraining, safety nets, labour law — needs rewriting now, not later.
The big pictureThe automation dividend is flowing to capital, not workers — and the data are starting to confirm it.

The full story

A study by Apollo Global Management, drawing on U.S. occupation-level data through mid-2025, found that workers in AI-exposed roles lost roughly 6.7 percentage points of real-wage growth relative to those in less-exposed jobs — with no statistically significant difference in employment rates. That asymmetry is the key signal: AI appears to be raising productivity while employers pocket the gains rather than sharing them through higher pay or shedding staff. Across the broader economy, inflation-adjusted wages fell 0.4% year-on-year through June, and labor's share of national output is near its lowest in decades. The evidence is still early — the study covers only a subset of occupations and causality is contested — but the pattern is consistent. The U.S. Department of Labor has launched AI literacy initiatives in response, and some analysts point to emerging AI-native roles as an offset. For policymakers watching the distribution of AI's gains, the wage channel is where the battle is quietly being fought.

AI Use in Classrooms Linked to Lower Test Scores, OECD Data Suggests

The OECD's 2025 PISA data, reported by Bloomberg, delivers an uncomfortable finding for ed-tech optimists: students who regularly use AI tools for schoolwork score lower on standardised tests than peers who do not. The gap is consistent across countries, suggesting it is not a fluke of one system. The problem appears to be how AI is being used, not that it is being used — passive outsourcing of thinking, rather than active learning with AI as a tutor.

Bloomberg
Why it mattersEvery Gulf ministry rolling out AI in classrooms now has a credibility problem — unless it can show the deployment is built around learning, not shortcuts.
The big pictureThe first large-scale international evidence on AI in schools is in — and it is a warning shot for every national ed-tech rollout.

The full story

The OECD's 2025 PISA cycle — the largest standardised education benchmark in the world, covering tens of thousands of students across dozens of countries — found a consistent pattern: students who use AI tools for schoolwork post lower test scores than those who do not. Bloomberg's reporting frames this as the first major cross-national dataset to put a number on AI's classroom impact, and the direction is the wrong one. Researchers caution that correlation is not causation — students who struggle may reach for AI as a crutch, inflating the apparent harm — but the scale of the pattern is hard to dismiss. The implicit lesson is about pedagogy: AI used for retrieval and shortcuts suppresses the effortful practice that builds durable knowledge. The finding lands just as UAE schools and Gulf universities are accelerating AI integration, and it gives policymakers a clear mandate — deployment without structured pedagogy and teacher training is a risk, not a shortcut to better outcomes.

Students who use AI generally score worse at school

The OECD's 2025 PISA data — the largest cross-country study of its kind — finds that students who use AI for schoolwork generally score lower than peers who don't, even after controlling for wealth. The damage is sharpest when AI is doing the cognitive work for students: summarising readings, drafting essays. But there is a meaningful exception: students trained to critically evaluate AI output, using it to support learning rather than replace thinking, outperform non-users. The signal is clear — deployment without pedagogy backfires.

The Verge
Why it mattersEvery ministry rolling out AI in classrooms needs a critical-evaluation curriculum alongside the tools — or the technology actively widens the gap.
The big pictureAI in schools is accelerating faster than the pedagogy needed to make it work — and the first global data now proves the cost.

The full story

The OECD's 2025 PISA round is the first globally comparable dataset on AI and student performance, and its headline finding is uncomfortable: frequent AI use correlates with lower scores in most countries. The harm is dose-dependent — daily users fare worst, while monthly or weekly users show comparatively modest dips. The mode of use matters just as much as the frequency: AI deployed as a shortcut (draft this essay, summarise this chapter) does consistent damage, whereas AI used as a research starting point or a study partner does less. The critical finding for policymakers is buried in the exception: students who received explicit training in evaluating AI output, and used the tools intentionally to deepen understanding, posted small but real gains over non-users. Access skews the picture too — AI use is far more common among wealthier, better-connected students, with adoption rates ranging from over 95% in Vietnam to roughly 60% in Japan. For Abu Dhabi, where school AI rollouts are already under way, the data make a direct case for pairing any deployment with structured critical-thinking curricula — without that layer, the tools risk becoming an expensive drag on attainment.
