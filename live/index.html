The $500B financing machine signals who actually controls the AI build-out

Three stories this issue point at the same structural shift: Nvidia signing MoUs with Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman and KKR to mobilise $500 billion in third-party infrastructure capital; MGX co-leading a $5 billion round into Databricks at a $190 billion valuation; and SK Hynix committing $720 billion to memory fabs. The pattern is not about chipmakers or model companies anymore. The constraint is now who can finance the physical stack. Sovereign and institutional capital is moving from passive LP positions into active co-construction of AI infrastructure — and the window to be a shaping partner, not a downstream customer, is narrowing fast.

The Desk
Why it mattersAbu Dhabi's sovereign funds are already in this game — but the Nvidia deal defines who sets the terms.
The big pictureWithin 18 months, the financing consortium around compute infrastructure will harden into a closed club — late entrants will pay a premium for access they could have priced today.

The full story

The headline number is $500 billion, but that is not the point. Nvidia's MoUs with Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR are not a fundraise — they are a governance document. Nvidia is deciding, right now, which pools of capital get to sit at the table when data centres are sited, when power contracts are struck, and when chip allocation decisions are made. The six partners named are not passive lenders; they are co-constructors. That is a different relationship, with different information rights and different returns.

The thing most observers are missing is that this is not a story about Nvidia needing money. At current revenue trajectory — OpenAI alone is running at over $40 billion annualised, doubling in under a year — the demand signal is unambiguous and the investment case is self-funding at scale. What Nvidia is actually doing is manufacturing a coalition of co-interested capital that will advocate for its infrastructure build-out across regulatory, planning and energy permitting systems worldwide. Wall Street's balance sheets are the political lubricant, not the fuel.

MGX's co-lead on the Databricks round is the correct Abu Dhabi instinct, and it deserves more credit than it is getting. Databricks sits at the layer where raw compute becomes usable enterprise AI — data pipelines, model training, analytics at $190 billion valuation. MGX is not buying exposure to a model; it is buying a seat in the data infrastructure that every large enterprise will run on. That is a more durable position than holding equity in any single frontier model company, because it is model-agnostic. Whatever wins the model race, the data layer underneath it stays.

The SK Hynix number — $720 billion in memory fab investment, with the Yongin Cluster starting production imminently — completes the picture. The AI stack has three hard physical constraints: compute (Nvidia), memory (Hynix and Samsung), and power. Two of the three are now locked into decade-long capital programmes controlled by a small number of actors. The third, power, remains the most open variable and therefore the most important one for Abu Dhabi to move on.

For a decision-maker in Abu Dhabi, there are two specific moves. First, the Nvidia consortium is the model: if Mubadala, MGX or ADIA are not already in conversations to be named co-financing partners on the next tranche of Gulf data centre build-out — with the governance rights that come with that, not just the yield — that conversation should begin this quarter. Being a customer of Nvidia's infrastructure is fine; being a co-financier with information rights and allocation preference is categorically different. Second, watch the power contracts. The one physical input not yet locked up by a concentrated consortium is energy. Abu Dhabi's renewable capacity and its existing relationships with hyperscalers make it one of very few jurisdictions that could offer a credible, low-carbon power guarantee to a data centre operator at the scale these programmes require. That is a lever, and it has a shelf life.

The bill is framed as a safety floor, not a ceiling, designed to preserve innovation while giving regulators a hard stop when things go wrong.
