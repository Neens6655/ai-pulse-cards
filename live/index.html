The ambition is to be the world's first fully AI-native government, pursued deliberately rather than on a fixed deadline, with human accountability baked into every layer.

The National (UAE)
Why it mattersTamm 5.0 is the clearest live test of what an AI-native government actually looks like in practice — not a vision document, a product launch.
The big pictureGovernments worldwide are racing to embed AI into public services — Abu Dhabi is betting it can set the template for what that looks like at scale.

The full story

Abu Dhabi's Department of Government Enablement will unveil Tamm 5.0 at GITEX Global in December, the latest iteration of the emirate's central government services app. The platform already handles transactions for 4.7 million users, with 90% completed within a single day and a cumulative saving of around 54 million hours annually — numbers that make it one of the highest-performing digital government platforms anywhere. Abu Dhabi isn't adopting AI — it's rebuilding government around it

Three Abu Dhabi moves landed in the same week: a Tamm 5.0 overhaul to make government proactive rather than reactive, an AiNative mass-upskilling programme for public servants, and Sheikh Tahnoon's AI council reviewing the UAE-US Campus and talent pipeline in a single sitting. Most observers will read these as separate announcements. They are one architectural decision: the state itself is being re-engineered as an AI-native institution. That is categorically different from any government that is merely deploying AI tools on top of existing structures.

The Desk
Why it mattersIf Tamm 5.0 works at scale, Abu Dhabi has a replicable sovereign-service OS that becomes an export — or a standard others chase.
The big pictureThe next contest is not who has the best AI tools, but whose government machinery is natively wired to use them — and Abu Dhabi is moving first.

The full story

Three Abu Dhabi stories dropped in the same 48-hour window this week, and the temptation is to file them separately: a new app version here, a training programme there, a council meeting somewhere else. That reading misses the point entirely. Taken together, they describe a single, deliberate decision to rebuild the machinery of government — not retrofit it.

Tamm 5.0, due at Gitex in December, is the sharpest signal. The current app already serves 4.7 million users, which is effectively the entire adult population of the emirate. The upgrade shifts the model from "citizen requests a service" to "government anticipates and delivers it." That is not a UX refresh — it is a philosophical inversion of how a state relates to its residents. Almost no city anywhere has achieved this at scale. Singapore's Singpass comes closest, and even it remains largely reactive. If Abu Dhabi pulls this off with 4.7 million live users as a baseline, it has built something genuinely without precedent.

The AiNative upskilling programme is what makes the infrastructure move credible. Tools do not run themselves; government employees do. The programme targets thousands of public servants — the people who will actually operate a proactive, AI-native Tamm — alongside the general public. Most governments that have deployed AI have done so over the heads of their workforces and wondered why adoption stalled. Abu Dhabi is sequencing this correctly: train the operators before you flip the switch.

What most people are missing is that these are not parallel initiatives — they are load-bearing on each other. Sheikh Tahnoon's AI council, reviewing the UAE-US Campus, research advances, and the talent pipeline in a single session, is the coordination mechanism that keeps them aligned. The UAE-US Campus is not just a data centre play; it is the compute and research backstop that gives Abu Dhabi the sovereign capability to run models it does not have to borrow from anyone. Tamm 5.0 is the citizen-facing application layer. AiNative is the human capital layer. These three pieces fit together into a stack, and the stack is the strategy.

For Abu Dhabi specifically, the window that is closing is the first-mover window on sovereign AI-native government services. Other Gulf states are watching. Saudi Arabia's Absher is a competent transactional platform, but it is not proactive. Qatar and Kuwait have nothing at comparable scale. If Tamm 5.0 launches on schedule in December and the early metrics are strong, Abu Dhabi will own the template — and with it, a legitimate claim to export the model, the codebase, or the methodology to partners across the region and beyond.

The decision a senior Abu Dhabi policymaker should be forcing now is not about Tamm's features. It is about measurement: what are the KPIs for "proactive government" that will be published at Gitex, and are they rigorous enough to be internationally credible? A vague demo will be dismissed. A 66% cost reduction — the number ServiceNow just posted for Raleigh's AI-run IT desk — is the kind of concrete outcome that travels. Abu Dhabi should be aiming for that level of specificity on day one. If it is, December becomes a genuine global moment.

This startup is issuing AI-generated acne prescriptions

A Utah startup called Nolla Health has built an app that scans a user's face, assesses acne severity, and generates a prescription — with two physicians approving every AI-written script for the first 100 patients, then shifting to spot-checks as the pilot scales to 500. The physician is no longer the author of the prescription — just the sign-off. That is a meaningful line crossed, and regulators and health ministries everywhere are watching how it lands.

The Verge
Why it mattersWhen AI writes the prescription and the doctor merely audits it, the entire model of clinical accountability shifts — and whoever sets the oversight rules first sets the global template.
The big pictureAutonomous AI prescribing is moving from science fiction to regulated pilot — and the oversight model that emerges here will shape healthcare delivery worldwide.

The full story

Nolla Health, a Utah-based startup, has launched one of the first real-world pilots in which an AI system — not a physician — originates a medical prescription. The app photographs a patient's face, scores acne severity, and outputs a treatment script. For the first 100 patients, two doctors approve each AI-generated prescription; beyond that, oversight drops to sampling as the program scales toward 500 patients. The deliberate tapering of physician involvement is the design, not a loophole — the company is explicitly stress-testing how much autonomous authority an AI can carry in a clinical setting. Dermatology and acne were chosen because the condition is well-defined and visually assessable, making it a tractable entry point. But the implications run far wider: if this model survives regulatory scrutiny in the US, it offers a replicable template for extending specialist-level care to populations with no access to dermatologists — a genuine opportunity for Gulf health systems serving underserved or remote communities.
