◆ Deep Dive02 / 24

Abu Dhabi is no longer just buying AI — it is selling it

Three stories this issue share a single logic: Abu Dhabi is converting domestic AI infrastructure into exportable product. TAMM Global licenses government-services architecture to foreign capitals. Jais 3 gives Arabic-speaking markets a sovereign language layer no Western model provides. M42 is taking its genomics-plus-AI clinical stack global. The Emirate has crossed from AI consumer to AI exporter. This is the shift that changes Abu Dhabi's position in the world — and it is happening faster than most observers have registered.

The Desk
Why it mattersThe window to capture government-tech and health-AI markets in the Global South is open now — rivals are not yet organised.
The big pictureIf the export stack scales, Abu Dhabi becomes the reference architecture for AI governance in the developing world within five years.

The full story

Three things happened in the same week that, read together, are more significant than any one of them alone. Abu Dhabi launched TAMM Global, a commercial entity that will own, operate and license its AI-unified government-services platform to other governments. MBZUAI and Inception42 released Jais 3 — six open Arabic-native models up to 375 billion parameters plus a dialect speech layer — aimed squarely at the forty-odd Arabic-speaking markets that no US lab has ever seriously prioritised. And M42 CEO Dimitris Moulavasilis confirmed that the G42 Healthcare spinout is exporting its genomics-and-AI clinical model, built in Abu Dhabi, into international markets. Separately, each is a product launch. Together, they are a posture.

The obvious read is that this is soft power: Abu Dhabi building influence through technology transfer the way China built it through infrastructure. That framing is not wrong, but it undersells the commercial logic. TAMM Global is not a grant programme — it licenses architecture and implementation methods. Jais 3 is open-weight, which means the moat is not the model itself but the ecosystem of fine-tuned applications, enterprise relationships and government endorsements that MBZUAI and Inception42 are best-placed to cultivate. M42 is not sharing research; it is selling clinical services. These are revenue-generating businesses aimed at markets — Southeast Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, the Arab world — that are being underserved by US and European providers who are either too expensive, too regulated or too culturally misaligned.

What most observers are missing is that the competitive window is narrow and the rivals are not yet organised. India has ambitions but no unified export vehicle for government AI. Singapore is small. China's AI export track record is uneven and, post-Huawei, carries sovereign-risk baggage for many buyers. For a government in East Africa or South Asia shopping for an AI-native public-services stack, TAMM Global has no obvious competitor today. Jais 3's Arabic-dialect speech capability is years ahead of anything a foreign lab will bother to build for a market this size. M42 is moving into geographies where world-class genomics infrastructure simply does not exist. First-mover advantages in government technology are especially durable — procurement cycles are long, switching costs are high, and the reference customer matters enormously.

For Abu Dhabi decision-makers, the implication is structural. The Emirate spent the last five years building AI capacity — compute via MGX and G42, talent via MBZUAI, clinical data via M42, citizen services via TAMM. The export turn means that investment now has a commercial return path beyond the domestic economy. But capturing that return requires moves that are different from what got Abu Dhabi here. Government-to-government relationships need to be converted into signed licensing deals before competitors wake up. Jais 3's open-weight release is smart, but Inception42 needs a professional services and integration business that can land and expand in target markets. M42's global expansion needs regulatory navigation infrastructure in every jurisdiction it enters — that is slow and expensive.

Watch three things: whether TAMM Global signs its first non-UAE government client within twelve months; whether Jais 3 attracts a critical mass of enterprise fine-tuners outside the Gulf; and whether M42's international revenues become material enough to appear in reporting ahead of any potential listing. Those three data points will tell you whether this week was a genuine strategic inflection or a very well-timed press cycle.

Microsoft has a new machine learning tool to improve weather forecasts

Microsoft has published open-source machine-learning software that makes weather forecasts two to six weeks ahead meaningfully more accurate — and far cheaper — than both traditional physics-based models and earlier AI approaches. The research, peer-reviewed in Nature, is designed not as a lab curiosity but as a practical tool for the people who need reliable long-range weather data most: farmers timing harvests, emergency teams pre-positioning resources, and city planners managing infrastructure. The weeks-out forecasting window is exactly where preparedness decisions get made — and where current models are weakest. Because it is open-source, any government or agency can deploy it without licensing a proprietary platform.

The Verge
Why it mattersBetter two-to-six-week forecasts are where disaster preparedness is won or lost — and this tool is free for any government to deploy.
The big pictureAI is quietly replacing decades-old physics models as the backbone of operational weather forecasting worldwide.

The full story

Microsoft's open-source release targets the "subseasonal" forecasting gap — the two-to-six-week window that is too far out for reliable daily forecasts but too near-term for seasonal climate models. That gap is where the most consequential preparedness calls get made: when to evacuate a coastline, how to stage emergency supplies, whether to delay a major infrastructure project. The new model outperforms both legacy numerical weather models and prior AI alternatives in that window, according to results published in Nature in October 2026. Because the code is open-source, it does not require a commercial contract — a public-works department or civil defence agency could integrate it directly into existing systems. For a region like the Gulf, where summer heat events, flash floods in wadis, and dust storms create real operational risk, sharper medium-range forecasting translates directly into lives and money saved. The model also runs at a fraction of the compute cost of traditional supercomputer-based simulations.
