Nvidia is becoming the financial backbone of the AI industry, not just its chipmaker

Nvidia is now writing billion-dollar cheques into SSI and backstopping a $250 billion OpenAI debt facility — in the same week. This is not a chipmaker diversifying; it is a supplier converting itself into a sovereign of the stack. The company that controls the hardware is now also setting the terms on which frontier labs get funded, which means it will increasingly decide who gets to build at scale and on what timeline.

The Desk
Why it mattersAbu Dhabi's chip access just improved; whether it can convert that into leverage before Nvidia locks in its preferred partners is the live question.
The big pictureThe race is no longer for chips alone — it is for the financial relationships that determine who Nvidia backs to build the next generation of frontier infrastructure.

The full story

Two stories this week look unrelated. They are the same story.

Nvidia has reportedly invested billions into Safe Superintelligence Inc., Ilya Sutskever's post-OpenAI company, in what SSI called a "long-term strategic partnership." In the same week, Nvidia and OpenAI are in advanced talks on a backstop of up to $250 billion — a credit guarantee that would allow OpenAI to raise the debt it needs to fund a 10-gigawatt AI data-centre campus in Pike County, Ohio. These are not portfolio plays. These are structural commitments. Nvidia is placing itself at the financial foundation of the two most consequential frontier AI projects on the planet.

The obvious read is that Nvidia is protecting its revenue: lock in the biggest compute buyers, secure the order books, keep H100 and Blackwell successors moving. That is true, but it is the smaller part of the story. What Nvidia is actually doing is converting chip dominance into capital dominance. A company that can guarantee $250 billion in debt, and simultaneously write a "strategic" cheque into the most credible safety-focused frontier lab, is no longer a component supplier. It is a node through which the entire industry's financing flows. That is a structurally different kind of power — and it is nearly impossible to replicate once the relationships are set.

The implication for the Broadcom-Apollo-Blackstone 20-gigawatt XPU platform announced this week is worth noting: a serious alternative compute stack is being assembled, with private capital behind it. But Nvidia's move is not about competing on chips. It is about making Nvidia the indispensable financial counterparty regardless of whose silicon runs in the rack.

For Abu Dhabi, the timing of the US chip export relaxation — framed explicitly as a reward for UAE support during the Iran conflict — is genuinely significant. The UAE is now being treated comparably to India, South Korea and Europe for chip access. That is a hard-won diplomatic position, and it opens a window. But the lesson from this week's news is that chip access is no longer the scarce resource. The scarce resource is the kind of trusted, strategic relationship with Nvidia that gets you inside the financial architecture — not just the hardware allocation. MGX, G42 and Mubadala have the capital; they now need to be asking whether they are in the room where Nvidia's long-term infrastructure commitments are being structured, or whether they are downstream customers who happen to have good export licences.

The decision a principal should be forcing this week is simple: who in Abu Dhabi has a direct relationship with Nvidia's investment and infrastructure leadership — not its sales team — and what would it take to be a co-guarantor or co-investor in the next Ohio-scale project, rather than a buyer of its outputs? The window between "UAE gets chip access" and "Nvidia's preferred partners are locked in for the decade" is not wide, and it is closing in real time.
