Dubai is not waiting for AI to mature — it is deploying it now, at scale

Two stories this issue look unrelated: Dubai using AI to monitor productivity across 32 government departments, and Dubai Chambers striking an agentic-AI deal with India's Nasscom. They are the same story. Dubai is treating AI deployment as an execution sport, not a strategy exercise — moving simultaneously on internal government efficiency and external talent pipelines, before most capitals have finished their AI strategies. The speed is the point.

The Desk
Why it mattersAbu Dhabi has the chips and the capital — the question is whether the deployment clock is running as fast as Dubai's.
The big pictureThe Gulf's AI race is no longer about who has the biggest compute — it is about who converts compute into running systems fastest.

The full story

Two stories in this issue look like routine government announcements. Read together, they are a signal about where the Gulf's internal AI competition is heading.

Dubai is deploying an AI-powered performance monitoring system across 32 government departments. The system links individual employee outputs to resource allocation, feeding a unified dashboard that senior planners use to adjust staffing and budget in near-real-time. This is not a pilot. It is a citywide rollout, applied simultaneously to the full breadth of Dubai's civil service. Most governments experimenting with AI productivity tools are running discrete proofs of concept in one ministry. Dubai is skipping that stage.

At the same time, Dubai Chambers signed a preliminary agreement with Nasscom — India's national tech industry body — to accelerate agentic AI adoption in the private sector. The practical mechanism: help Indian technology firms, the world's largest pool of AI implementation talent, establish a UAE presence. This is smart sourcing. Rather than waiting for a domestic agentic-AI ecosystem to emerge organically, Dubai is importing the builders.

The thing most observers are missing is that these two moves are architecturally connected. The government productivity system creates a visible, working deployment — a reference case that private-sector firms can point to when pitching agentic solutions to regional clients. The Nasscom pipeline ensures the implementation talent exists to replicate and extend it. Dubai is building a flywheel: government as anchor customer, India as talent engine, the private sector as scale multiplier.

This matters for Abu Dhabi for a specific reason. The emirate holds assets Dubai does not: Stargate UAE's 1 GW supercomputing complex (cost projection north of $30 billion), MGX's balance sheet, and G42's model relationships with frontier labs. That is a formidable compute and capital position. But compute without deployment is infrastructure sitting idle. The Goldman Sachs labor-market data in this issue is instructive — AI is already visibly squeezing employment in call centres, software publishing and management consulting in developed economies. The displacement is happening now, not in a future planning horizon. Governments that have working deployments in place will be in a position to reshape their public sectors ahead of that pressure; those still writing strategies will be reacting to it.

The replicable move for Abu Dhabi is not the technology — it is the sequencing. Pick a department with measurable, quantifiable outputs (infrastructure permitting, customs clearance, health referrals) and deploy an AI monitoring and optimisation layer there completely, not partially. Make it the reference case. Then use that case to accelerate private-sector adoption by showing that the government itself is a working customer. The Nasscom model is also directly replicable: Abu Dhabi Global Market and the Department of Economic Development both have the convening authority to run a structured inbound program for agentic-AI implementers from India, Southeast Asia, or Eastern Europe.

The window is not permanently open. Dubai's head start in deployment — even a six-month lead in working government systems — translates into data advantages, institutional habits and vendor relationships that compound. Watch whether Abu Dhabi's announced federal AI-agent rollouts move from announcement to live system before the end of Q4. That is the metric that matters.

Goldman studied where AI is squeezing labor markets. Here's what it found

Goldman Sachs has put numbers on what many suspected: AI is already reshaping hiring in developed economies, not as a future threat but as a present one. In sectors like call centers, software publishing, management consulting, and advertising, employment is running measurably below its pre-AI trend — with the slowdown in job openings dating to late 2022. Entry-level workers are bearing the brunt, and the effect is visible across Germany, Australia, and the U.S., where roughly 15–20% of firms have now adopted AI tools.

CNBC
Why it mattersWhen Goldman puts a trend line on it, policymakers can no longer call it anecdotal — workforce strategy needs to catch up now.
The big pictureAI adoption has crossed the threshold from productivity experiment to structural labor-market force — and the data is starting to show it.

The full story

Goldman Sachs researchers tracked employment trends across industries in developed economies and found a clear pattern: sectors most exposed to automation — call centers, software publishing, management consulting, advertising — are adding workers more slowly than historical trends would predict, with the divergence beginning in late 2022, roughly when large language models entered mainstream use. The finding is significant because it moves the conversation from projection to measurement: this is happening now, not in a decade. Entry-level roles are the most affected, as AI handles the task bundles that have traditionally served as career on-ramps. Adoption is estimated at 15–20% across developed economies — still a minority, but already large enough to leave a statistical fingerprint on hiring data. For Gulf policymakers designing national workforce and Emiratisation strategies, the implication is direct: the entry-level pipeline is the first to be disrupted, and the window to retrain and redirect young workers is narrowing.

China AI firms tap Nvidia power overseas as U.S. weighs crackdown

China's biggest AI labs — ByteDance, Alibaba, Tencent — are training advanced models on Nvidia chips they don't legally own, by renting compute remotely through data centres in Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, and Japan. U.S. export controls ban selling the chips to Chinese entities, but say nothing about cloud access. The loophole is the gap between owning a chip and running one. Congress is now weighing the Remote Access Security Act to close it, though industry pushback and definitional fights are slowing progress.

CNBC
Why it mattersExport controls that ban chip ownership but not cloud access are paper walls — and Southeast Asia's data-centre boom is the proof.
The big pictureThe next frontier of the chip war is not hardware shipments — it is who can access compute remotely, and from where.

The full story

U.S. export rules have spent three years trying to keep advanced Nvidia chips out of Chinese hands — but "hands" is doing a lot of work. Chinese hyperscalers have quietly shifted to renting GPU time through cloud providers in Southeast Asia, where the chips sit legally and access flows freely across borders. The result: ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent are training frontier models on restricted hardware without touching it. The Bureau of Industry and Security and White House officials say they are aware of the practice and are exploring faster-moving rules to close the gap. The proposed Remote Access Security Act would require cloud providers to verify who their customers are and block access for prohibited parties — but the scope fight is fierce: which compute counts, who is banned, and how far KYC obligations extend are all unresolved. Southeast Asia's data-centre construction boom, much of it backed by Gulf and U.S. capital, is now directly implicated in where this lands.

EdSurge
Why it mattersAny Gulf ministry building a national edtech procurement standard now has a ready-made framework to adapt — or leapfrog.
The big pictureGovernments worldwide are shifting from "how much tech?" to "does it actually work?" — and procurement rules are the enforcement mechanism.

The full story

The U.S. Department of Education released long-awaited guidance in August 2026 telling states and school districts how to evaluate, procure, and sustain classroom technology. The core message: screen time is the wrong metric. Districts should require vendors to provide independent evidence — including randomised controlled trials where available — that their tools actually improve student learning before a purchase order is signed. Vendors, in turn, are urged to design with teacher and parent input, publish objective outcome data, and support professional development rather than just selling licences. The guidance gives particular attention to students with disabilities, rural learners, and home-bound students, where well-deployed edtech can close real gaps. No new federal rules were created; states and districts hold the implementation lever, which means the guidance's bite will depend on local will. For any government designing a national school-AI strategy, the framework offers a practical checklist: distinguish recreational from instructional use, evaluate on outcomes, and build in continuous review cycles.
